Give appearance a purpose

Connect a message, an intended feeling, and visual evidence.

A beautiful image can hide the service. Start with the idea your audience needs to understand.

In 1 minute

  1. Message: preserve a cherished garment
  2. Intended feeling: care
  3. Evidence: an actual repair detail

1Art direction chooses what to communicate

Art direction organizes visual choices before production.

Isa, a 35-year-old designer, helps Célia, a 57-year-old seamstress.

The fictional Fio Claro shop repairs clothes with visible mending.

The challenge is to show care without promising the wear will disappear.

The message will be to preserve a cherished garment.

The intended feeling will be care.

The evidence will be a detail of the repair.

A generated photo can illustrate this idea.

To advertise completed work, use real, authorized photos of that service.

Isa replaces the request for sophistication with a photo that makes the repair visible.

Open the worksheet and map to copy · Educational references for the case.

2Separate intention from outcome

Write three lines in a notes app: message, feeling, and evidence.

Then choose a simple action, such as asking someone to assess the garment.

The feeling is the creator’s intention. Only feedback from people shows how they received the image.

Call this set of repeatable rules Visual DNA, or a visual identity system.

It guides color, type, composition, and photography.

It is not a scientific test of brand personality.

It also does not guarantee trust or sales.

Célia can tell the illustrative image from a photo of work actually delivered.

3Check the decision against its use

Show the repair, explain the care, and invite people to have the garment assessed.

Célia checks the worksheet: evidence—a detail of the actual repair.

Test yourself

Does a generated sewing image prove the shop completed a repair?

If you got stuck here, that is normalUse the fictional case if you do not have a brand yet. Write down your assumption beside the evidence you will need to produce.

You can write a brand core that guides choices.

Loose adjectives lead to different choices in every piece. A short worksheet keeps you from starting the conversation over.

In 1 minute

  1. Audience and need: cherished, worn garments
  2. Offer and distinction: explained visible mending
  3. Evidence: documented real process

1Start with the audience, need, and offer

Open the note from the previous lesson.

Write who you serve, what need you meet, and what you offer.

For Fio Claro: people who want to keep wearing cherished clothes; repair wear; assess and perform visible mending.

This example does not represent all seamstresses.

Add why someone might choose the shop and what evidence supports that reason.

The proposed distinction is to connect the repair with the garment’s story.

Future proof will be a real series of detail shots, with an explanation of the work.

Observe perception without leading the answer

You can prepare a neutral question and separate observations from assumptions.

Asking whether a brand seems caring gives away the answer you want. Find out what people understood.

In 1 minute

  1. Question: open, with no suggested adjective
  2. Record: keep the person’s words separate from your interpretation
  3. Status: feedback collected or pending

1Ask before you explain

Show a piece and ask: What do you think this place does?

Then ask which detail led you to that impression.

Do not explain the concept first.

Keep the person’s words and the test context, with permission to record them.

A short conversation offers clues; it does not represent the whole audience.

Do not make up answers to fill in the worksheet.

If no one is available, prepare the script and mark feedback as pending.

This lesson’s short practice ends with the script; actual research comes later.

Isa writes down the feedback before suggesting a photography change.

2Separate words, interpretation, and decision

Record three fields: what I heard, what I interpreted, and what I will test.

If someone says “new clothes,” the image may be hiding the repair.

Your interpretation is still an assumption.

Try a larger detail and ask the question again.

The goal is not to make everyone like it.

It is to check whether people understood the main message.

Do not archive all your old communication based on one response.

Keep versions so you can compare the effect of changing one thing at a time.

Célia prepares the questions and marks feedback as pending until she can talk with someone.

3Check the decision against its use

What does this place do, and what detail made you think so?

Célia checks the worksheet: status—feedback collected or pending.

If you got stuck here, that is normalYou do not need to find someone now. Prepare the script; an imagined conversation does not count as collected feedback.

Turn three pillars into visible choices

You can define three pillars, each with a purpose, feeling, and distinction.

Words like authentic or human can describe almost any image. Give each word a visible consequence.

In 1 minute

  1. Care: show the finish
  2. Continuity: keep the garment recognizable
  3. Clarity: choose one main focus

1Three pillars, three commitments

A pillar is an idea that guides several pieces.

For Fio Claro, use care, continuity, and clarity.

Care calls for visible texture and finish.

Continuity shows the whole garment, still usable.

Clarity organizes the service and the next step.

Separate purpose, intended feeling, and distinction.

The purpose is to explain the repair.

The intended feeling is ease.

The distinction is valuing visible mending.

This forms a concept: keep wearing it, with the repair becoming part of the story.

Isa rejects a blur that hides the stitches, even though she likes the atmosphere.

2A rule must help you reject something

For each pillar, write one choice and one rejection.

Clarity allows one main focus and rejects five competing calls to action.

Care allows a sharp detail and rejects softening that erases the stitches.

A rule should help when two appealing options come up.

You do not need to repeat the same framing.

A wide photo and a detail shot can follow the same pillar.

Consistency comes from the reason and the features you keep.

Keep examples that support the rule and examples that contradict it.

Célia asks for a wide photo when the detail makes the garment hard to recognize.

3Check the decision against its use

Show the repair texture, keep the garment’s context, and highlight one action.

Célia checks the worksheet: clarity—choose one main focus.

Test yourself

Find a difference without copying competitors

You can record patterns and a possible distinction across three references.

Being different in every way can make the service harder to recognize. First find what repeats and why.

In 1 minute

  1. Sample: three references with URL and date
  2. Pattern: a feature you actually observed
  3. Assumption: a distinction that keeps the offer clear

1Observe what is there

Choose three public pages from the same category.

Record the URL, date, and two visible elements: photo subject, dominant color, amount of text, or type of proof.

Describe what you see.

Do not claim intent or sales results without evidence.

Do not copy logos, phrases, or entire layouts.

Research helps you recognize conventions and decide what to test.

For the fictional case, suppose the references show only finished clothes.

Showing the process becomes a possible distinction, not a proven discovery.

Isa spots a pattern in the sample without calling it a rule for the whole market.

2Look beyond your category for references

A tool catalog can teach you how to show details.

An exhibition can teach you how to organize a sequence.

Apply the principle to your problem without copying the full look.

Record which element you will use and which you will leave out.

A useful distinction still explains the offer.

An unusual palette cannot make up for a confusing photo.

Also check whether you can repeat the choice with the resources you have.

A direction that depends on unworkable production may fail the following week.

Célia looks for a simple way to show the process with the light she already has.

Record visible patterns and test a distinction tied to the offer.

Build a mood board that guides choices

You can organize six references into three groups with captions.

A board full of beautiful images can point in opposite directions. Each reference needs a purpose.

In 1 minute

  1. Subject: full garment and detail
  2. Light: visible texture without excessive shine
  3. Composition: space for a call to action

1Three groups keep references organized

A reference board, also called a mood board, gathers images to guide the work.

Make three groups: subject, light, and composition.

Use two references per group.

Under each image, write which feature you will use and which you will not copy.

In the subject group, Fio Claro combines a full garment view with a repair detail.

For light, choose soft window light and shadows that reveal texture.

For composition, leave room for a short call to action.

A reference can be your own fabric photo or a simple drawing.

Isa separates the lighting from the composition so she does not copy an entire photo.

Open the worksheet and map to copy · Educational references for the case.

2A caption turns inspiration into a rule

Write observable statements: preserve the texture, move closer to the stitch, leave an area clear.

Do not just list words like welcoming and premium.

The board should let someone else make a similar choice without guessing your taste.

Arrange the groups in a document or presentation app you already use.

Add the images and a caption under each one.

Record the source of external references.

For public sharing, use your own or authorized images, or link to the source.

Célia takes her own photo when she cannot find a reference with a clear source.

3Check the decision against its use

Separate subject, light, and composition, with two annotated references per group.

Célia checks the worksheet: composition—space for a call to action.

If you got stuck here, that is normalYou can start with simple phone photos. The caption should explain the choice, not praise the image.

Give each visual element a purpose

You can fill in a map with six elements and hypotheses about what they mean.

The same color can communicate different things in different situations. Avoid a universal dictionary of emotions.

In 1 minute

  1. Map: six elements with a purpose
  2. Association: a context-based hypothesis
  3. Test: an observation that could challenge the choice

1Purpose first, association second

The meaning map connects an element, its purpose, the desired association, and a test.

Example: an off-white background separates the repair and suggests natural material; test it in the full design.

The association is a hypothesis.

It depends on context, the combination, and the person looking at it.

Fill in six rows: background color, text color, accent, shape, texture, and photography.

Add composition or typography when it makes sense.

The rule does not need to work for every brand.

It needs to fit your offering and the pieces you plan to make.

Isa uses blue for text and rust for small details.

Open the worksheet and map to copy · Educational references for the case.

2Color has a role, not magic powers

For Fio Claro, off-white fills the background, deep blue supports readability, and rust marks details.

We are not claiming that blue automatically builds trust.

Each color’s role helps keep the layout consistent.

Check readability in the next lesson.

If two elements do the same job, simplify.

If an ornament does not help the message, try removing it.

Keep an earlier version for comparison.

The map should also say what to observe to confirm or reject the hypothesis.

Célia removes an ornament that competed with the repair and made the service harder to explain.

3Check the decision against its use

Deep blue will be used for text; check readability and perception.

Célia uses the sheet to check: test: an observation that could challenge the choice.

Test yourself

What can you say about a chosen color before testing it?

It is normal to get stuck hereUse short phrases. If you are unsure of the association, mark it as an open hypothesis and describe only the purpose.

Make the message work at a small size

You can check the hierarchy and contrast of a headline.

A design may work when enlarged but become unreadable on a phone. Test it at the size people will see it.

In 1 minute

  1. Order: headline, explanation, and action
  2. Contrast: aim for at least 4.5 to 1 for regular text
  3. Use: read it at actual phone size

1Decide what people read first

Choose a main headline, an explanation, and an action.

Use size, weight, and spacing to distinguish their roles.

Two typeface families are enough for this exercise.

One may work, too.

Keep the accents and test real Portuguese words.

For Fio Claro, the headline is Your clothes can carry on.

The explanation is Visible repair assessment.

The action is Bring in your item.

Do not hide the offer in tiny text.

People need to understand the service before deciding what to do next.

Isa checks the contrast of the color pair before using it in the design.

Open the contrast checker. Use the Foreground Color (text) and Background Color (background) fields.

2Measure contrast, then check readability

For regular text, use a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5 to 1 as a reference.

A checker compares the text color with the background.

Open the checker listed in the extra materials.

Enter both colors and read the result.

Try blue text #203448 on an off-white background #F4EFE5.

Apply the pair to your design, or assess the example shown in this lesson.

Record which option you tested and its measured ratio.

For your own colors, measure the actual pair.

A solid strip makes it easier to check text over a photo.
